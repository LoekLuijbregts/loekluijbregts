Your Business Is Probably More Valuable Than Your Proposition

Many strong businesses do not have a value problem. They have a translation problem. Customers experience something valuable that the website, pitch and offer barely explain.

By Loek Luijbregts · Published 18 August 2026 · 8 minute read

A filmmaker came to me while looking for a €100.000 investor. He believed the business needed outside capital to take the next step. In return, he was prepared to give away part of the company.

The business did not first need an investor. It needed a clearer understanding of what his best customers were already willing to pay for.

We uncovered the value hidden inside his work, translated it into a stronger offer and made it easier to see, show and sell. He kept 100% ownership. Within nine months from day 0, the collaboration had returned 10x its investment.

The inside of the business is not the market

Inside a business, people naturally talk about what they do. They discuss services, features, processes, history and expertise. All of that may be true. It is rarely the easiest way for a customer to recognise value.

The customer sees a different business. They remember what changed, what became easier, what risk disappeared and why they trusted one company over another.

This creates a common value gap:

  • The business explains its activities.
  • The customer values the resulting change.
  • The proposition connects the two poorly.

When that gap is large, a business may look interchangeable online and exceptional after one conversation. Founders often respond by adding more copy. The result is usually a longer website that still leaves the important truth hidden.

A five-question Outside-In value audit

You do not need a six-month branding process to see whether this is happening. Start with five questions.

1. What becomes meaningfully better?

Do not answer with the deliverable. “A strategy,” “a film” or “a workshop” is what you produce. Ask what the customer can decide, achieve or avoid because the work exists.

2. Who experiences the most value?

Your ideal customer is not simply the organisation with enough budget. It is the customer whose situation makes your specific strengths unusually valuable.

3. What do your best customers mention without being prompted?

Repeated phrases are evidence. If several customers mention speed, confidence, fresh perspective, introductions or relief, those words deserve more attention than the language invented during an internal positioning meeting.

4. What only becomes clear after a conversation?

Write down the sentence that regularly creates the “now I understand” moment. That sentence probably belongs much earlier in your proposition.

5. What proof makes the value easy to believe?

A clear promise without proof still creates risk. Proof may be a commercial result, customer quote, demonstration, press placement, rebooking, before-and-after example or respected third party choosing to participate.

The value gap in one line

What insiders describe is often not the same as what ideal customers value. Your proposition needs to connect both using language and proof customers can recognise.

Three expensive symptoms of hidden value

Every sale needs the founder. The website creates interest, but only the founder can explain why the offer matters. This limits scale and makes the proposition dependent on one person.

Price conversations start too early. When customers cannot see a meaningful difference, they compare inputs and rates. Clear value changes the basis of comparison.

The best work arrives by accident. Ideal projects come through referrals, while public communication attracts broader and less valuable requests. The market cannot request work it cannot see.

Value activation is more than better wording

Finding the value is only the first step. It needs to become usable across the business.

  1. Positioning: what you want the right people to understand.
  2. Proposition: what they can actually buy.
  3. Proof: why they should believe it.
  4. Activation: where they experience or encounter that proof.
  5. Commercial capture: how attention becomes a conversation, lead, sale or long-term relationship.

This is why overlooked value activation is not a writing exercise. It connects customer research, strategic choices, story, proof and practical commercial movement.

A useful first action

Look at the last three projects you would happily do again. For each one, write down the result the customer received, the part of the work they valued most and the proof that result was real.

Then compare those answers with the first screen of your website.

If the valuable truth only appears in your memory, invoices or customer conversations, your business may already be more valuable than your proposition.
