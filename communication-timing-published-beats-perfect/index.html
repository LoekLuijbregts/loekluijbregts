Communication timing, distribution and desired action · Communication strategy and execution

Published at the Right Moment Beats Perfected Too Late

Good communication is successful transfer, desired reception and a useful next action. The message only creates value when it reaches the right person while the opportunity is still alive.

By Loek Luijbregts · Published 19 August 2026 · 11 minute read

Some communication is beautifully written, carefully approved and commercially useless.

Nothing inside it is obviously wrong. The message is clear. The design is strong. Every stakeholder had an opportunity to improve it.

It simply arrived after the customer had decided, after the journalist had filed, after the event conversation had moved on or after the market stopped caring.

At the moment supreme, the communication that is available counts. The perfect version waiting in somebody’s review queue does not.

A working definition

Good communication is successful transfer, desired reception and a useful next action. Quality matters, but quality without delivery, reception or timing is unfinished work.

Communication is an effect, not only an artefact

Teams regularly judge communication by inspecting the artefact: the strategy, briefing, article, film, press release, deck or website.

The receiver experiences a different chain:

  1. the communication becomes available;
  2. it reaches them through a channel they use;
  3. it earns enough attention to be noticed;
  4. they interpret its intended meaning;
  5. they believe enough of it;
  6. they know what to do next;
  7. and the next action is still possible.

A failure anywhere in that chain can make a good message ineffective.

Lasswell’s five questions still expose weak communication

Harold Lasswell’s influential 1948 formulation asked: who says what, in which channel, to whom, with what effect?

The model is simple, linear and incomplete for modern interactive communication. It remains useful because teams often optimise “what” while leaving “channel,” “to whom” and “effect” vague.

A launch team may polish the announcement while failing to answer:

  • Who is the most credible sender?
  • Which part of the message matters to each receiver?
  • Where will that person realistically encounter it?
  • What observable effect should happen next?

If you cannot answer those questions, more copy editing will not complete the communication system.

Shannon helps explain why being published is not the same as being received

Claude Shannon’s 1948 mathematical theory of communication was about reliable information transmission, not marketing persuasion. Its language of source, signal, channel, receiver and noise still offers a valuable lens.

Business communication travels through noise:

  • competing posts and headlines;
  • an overflowing inbox;
  • internal terminology the customer does not use;
  • a weak preview image;
  • an algorithm that shows the message to only part of the audience;
  • a slow page that delays the proof;
  • or a call to action that asks for too much, too soon.

This is why sending once is not a complete distribution strategy. Important truth often needs responsible redundancy: several formats, credible senders and well-chosen channels repeating the same core meaning without becoming repetitive noise themselves.

The Greeks had a word for the right moment

Kairos describes the opportune or fitting moment. It is different from simply measuring time. It asks whether this is the moment in which a message can create movement.

Research on kairos in rhetoric describes right timing and due measure as integral to persuasive action. More recent media research argues that algorithmic platforms operate around their own forms of “right-time,” deciding which content becomes visible in rapidly changing feeds. See The Right-Time Web.

Journalism makes this especially visible. Research across more than a century of journalism textbooks found that timeliness has remained one of the recurring news values, alongside impact, proximity, prominence and other context-dependent factors. A true story may still be less newsworthy next week because the open attention window has closed.

GRAVAA did not create the moment. We were ready for it.

GRAVAA developed an adaptable tyre-pressure system that riders could adjust while cycling. The product was technically interesting. Technical interest alone did not guarantee broad attention.

When Marianne Vos won the 2024 Gravel World Championships using the technology, the market already cared about the race, the winner and the question of competitive advantage. The proof, respected athlete and public moment came together.

Our communication could connect the result to an understandable product story while journalists, riders, partners and investors were actively asking what had happened.

The resulting earned attention contributed to more than 60 million impressions, international coverage, a tripled social audience and thousands of new subscribers with no advertising spend. Read the full GRAVAA case study.

The communication still needed accurate facts, photographs, product explanations, media relationships and a useful place for attention to go. But a version delivered three weeks later would not have carried the same value, even if every sentence had been improved.

The timing advantage

Perfect timing does not rescue weak or untrue communication. It multiplies communication that is credible, prepared and available when the receiver already has a reason to care.

Published beats perfected under four conditions

“Just ship it” is not a responsible universal rule. Publishing the available version is the better choice when four conditions are met.

  1. The core claim is true. The facts, names, figures and implications are defensible.
  2. The downside is controlled. The message does not create unacceptable legal, safety, privacy or reputational risk.
  3. The next step works. A reader can find the proof, request information, subscribe, buy or contact the right person.
  4. The attention window is open now. Delay will reduce relevance, access or the probability of reception.

When these conditions are absent, speed can become recklessness. Medical guidance, financial claims, crisis statements, contracts and irreversible accusations require different levels of review.

The goal is not lower standards. It is a better distinction between improvements that protect truth and improvements that merely make a team more comfortable.

Build readiness before the moment arrives

The easiest way to communicate quickly is to avoid creating everything from zero during the opportunity.

Build a readiness layer:

  • a current fact sheet and approved product explanation;
  • high-quality photographs in usable web and press formats;
  • short biographies and quotable founder viewpoints;
  • customer proof with permission to publish;
  • a list of relevant journalists, creators, partners and customers;
  • landing pages and calls to action that already work;
  • clear rules for what needs approval and who can give it.

This is why the launch is too late to start building proof. Preparation protects quality and timing at the same time.

Use a three-speed publishing system

Speed 1: Moment communication

Publish within hours while attention is active. Use verified facts, one clear meaning, a relevant asset and one useful next step. Improve or expand later.

Speed 2: Useful follow-up

Publish within days. Add interpretation, customer implications, behind-the-scenes context and answers to the questions the first communication created.

Speed 3: Compounding reference

Publish the durable case study, guide or search-focused article. This version can be deeper, better sourced and designed to remain useful after the immediate attention has passed.

The three speeds prevent a false choice between fast and good. The first captures the moment. The second serves the conversation. The third builds a reusable business asset.

Reach is probabilistic, so design for more than one chance

Even strong communication can reach fewer intended receivers than you hoped. Social feeds distribute unevenly. Journalists have limited space. Customers miss emails. Search visibility grows slowly. People are busy.

Do not interpret one weak distribution result as proof that the message has no value. Repackage the core truth intelligently:

  • a founder post that explains the judgment;
  • a customer email focused on relevance;
  • a media pitch focused on public interest;
  • a short video showing the proof;
  • a searchable article that remains available;
  • a sales asset used when the question naturally appears.

Efficiency is not publishing only once. Efficiency is building one truthful source from which several well-placed transfers can be made.

Judge communication by reception and movement

After publishing, look beyond output metrics. Ask:

  • Did the intended receiver encounter it?
  • What did they understand or repeat?
  • Did the visual and wording create the intended frame?
  • Which questions or objections remained?
  • What useful next action occurred?
  • Was the timing early enough to influence the decision?

The answers improve the next transfer. Communication becomes a learning system rather than a sequence of finished assets.

The moment supreme has no review queue

Communication deserves craft. It also deserves a deadline connected to reality.

When the timing is good, published communication can outperform communication perfected after the reason to care has disappeared. When the timing is exceptional, availability becomes part of quality.

Prepare the truth. Understand the receiver. Choose the channel. Reduce the noise. Make the next action work. Then publish while the moment can still carry the message.
