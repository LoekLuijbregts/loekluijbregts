Customer Reviews Are Not Testimonials. They Are Unpaid Strategy Research

Most businesses collect reviews to prove they are trustworthy. The same reviews can also reveal what customers value, why they buy and where future growth may be hiding.

By Loek Luijbregts · Published 18 August 2026 · 7 minute read

A positive review is usually thanked, copied into a testimonial carousel and forgotten.

That wastes most of its value.

Reviews contain unprompted customer language. They show what people remembered strongly enough to publish. Across dozens or hundreds of reviews, repeated themes can expose a gap between the company insiders think they run and the experience customers actually value.

This makes reviews useful for far more than reassurance. They can influence positioning, offers, product decisions, internal culture and the stories a business deserves to tell more confidently.

Why reviews are strategically different

Internal teams describe the business using categories they work with every day. Customers describe moments.

They mention the employee who solved something unexpectedly, the feeling of arriving, the detail that removed risk, the speed of response or the tradition they now associate with a product.

These details are not polished. That is their strength. They are close to the language future customers may use when recognising themselves.

Five signals to extract

1. The job customers hired you to do

What was happening in their life or business? A product may be bought for one functional reason and valued for a very different emotional or social reason.

2. The change they remember

Look for before-and-after language: confused to confident, difficult to easy, ordinary to memorable, risky to trusted.

3. The unexpected value

Phrases such as “what surprised me,” “I did not expect” and “the best part was” often reveal strengths missing from the official proposition.

4. The source of trust

Did people mention a recommendation, family history, employee, demonstration, press story, atmosphere or customer service moment?

5. The friction

Negative and mixed reviews reveal what almost prevented value from being experienced. The goal is not to argue with them. It is to identify repeated barriers the business can remove.

A simple review sheet

Create columns for starting situation, valued change, exact words, trust signal, unexpected value and friction. Code 30 to 50 reviews before drawing conclusions.

What happened at Schrobbelèr

Schrobbelèr is a family-owned business with a huge following. The brand already has history, affection and customer energy. That can make untapped potential harder to see, not easier.

During two in-company marketing inspiration sessions, customer reviews became an Outside-In mirror. Instead of starting with new campaign ideas, we looked at what customers were already saying and what those statements suggested about loyalty, experience and future opportunity.

The reviews helped move the conversation from “what should we post?” toward “what do people already love, and how could the business activate more of that value?”

Turn repeated language into choices

A theme is useful only when it changes something.

  • Positioning: move a repeatedly valued outcome into the main story.
  • Website: organise proof around customer situations instead of a random quote slider.
  • Product: protect details customers consistently describe as essential.
  • Content: tell the stories behind the moments people keep mentioning.
  • Training: show employees which behaviours create disproportionate customer value.
  • Innovation: explore repeated requests or frustrations as possible future propositions.

Do not let the loudest review become the strategy

Review research has limits. Customers who publish reviews are self-selecting. Platforms may overrepresent very positive or very negative experiences. Older reviews may describe a business that has changed.

Use reviews to form hypotheses, then compare them with interviews, sales conversations, rebooking data and behaviour.

Also protect specificity. “Great service” is not yet an insight. Which part was great? At what moment? Compared with what expectation? The strategic value lives in the details.

A two-hour starting point

  1. Collect the 50 most recent meaningful reviews.
  2. Remove only duplicates and obvious spam.
  3. Mark repeated outcomes, emotions and trust signals.
  4. Select five phrases that sound unlike your current marketing.
  5. Discuss what would change if those customers were right.

The most valuable result may not be new copy. It may be a service worth protecting, a proposition worth testing or an opportunity the company had become too familiar to notice.

Your customers may have written the strategy already

Businesses often pay for external research while a large body of customer evidence sits publicly available and almost unread.

Reviews do not make the decision for you. They help ensure the decision responds to real value rather than internal assumptions.
