Founder-Led LinkedIn: Write It Yourself or Use a Ghostwriter?

The founder must own the truth. That does not mean the founder must type every sentence. The right writing model protects both authenticity and consistency.

By Loek Luijbregts · Published 12 February 2026 · Updated 18 August 2026 · 9 minute read

Founder-led writing works because customers want access to judgment. They want to understand how the person behind the business sees the market, makes decisions and responds when the obvious answer is incomplete.

That access cannot be manufactured from generic thought-leadership templates.

But insisting that a busy founder personally writes every word creates another problem: strong ideas remain trapped in meetings, voice notes and half-finished drafts.

Authenticity does not mean typing alone

The useful distinction is ownership. The founder must own the experience, opinion, evidence and final meaning. Another person can help extract, organise, challenge and edit those ingredients.

A speechwriter does not need to invent the leader’s beliefs. A skilled interviewer does not replace the expert. The value is translation.

The non-negotiable

The founder owns the truth. The writer owns the translation process. The audience should never be asked to believe a life, lesson or opinion that is not real.

Three models that can work

1. The founder writes

This is best when writing gives the founder energy, their natural voice is already clear and they can maintain a useful rhythm. An editor may still tighten structure, remove repetition and protect the strongest sentence.

The danger is inconsistency. Many founders publish three good posts during a motivated week and disappear for two months.

2. The founder speaks, an editor shapes

A weekly 30-minute interview can produce several strong drafts. The editor asks for examples, notices phrases with energy and returns a version the founder can correct quickly.

This is my preferred middle ground for many leaders. It preserves spontaneous language while removing the burden of starting from a blank page.

3. A trusted ghostwriter runs the system

The best CEO ghostwriters are not anonymous content factories. They are perceptive interviewers with business judgment. They learn the founder’s language, notice contradictions, understand what cannot be published and know when a draft is technically polished but emotionally false.

This model fits leaders with valuable access and very limited writing time. It requires trust, proximity and a clear approval process.

Five questions before hiring a ghostwriter

  1. Where will the raw material come from? Real conversations, decisions and field notes are better than invented prompts.
  2. How will they learn my voice? Ask to see the interview and revision process, not only final posts.
  3. Can they challenge me? A valuable writer asks for proof and pushes past safe generalities.
  4. What will never be fabricated? Personal experiences, customer outcomes and opinions need explicit boundaries.
  5. Who owns the archive? Interviews, transcripts, drafts and published ideas should remain useful business assets.

Red flags in founder content

Borrowed vulnerability. A dramatic personal story appears because the format performs well, not because the founder wants to share it.

Universal certainty. Every post ends with a grand rule even when the founder’s real view is conditional.

Voice by adjective. The brief says “bold, inspiring and provocative,” but contains no examples of how the founder actually speaks.

Engagement without consequence. The writing attracts peers who applaud but never helps an ideal customer understand the business.

A better test

Read the draft aloud. Would you say it to a respected customer across the table? Would your team recognise the judgment as yours? Can you defend the claim with a real example?

A six-week pilot

Do not begin with an annual content contract. Test the working relationship.

  1. Record one positioning interview and one customer-story interview.
  2. Create a short voice guide using phrases the founder actually uses.
  3. Publish twice a week for six weeks.
  4. Track qualified replies, customer recognition and useful sales conversations.
  5. Review which ideas created trust, not only which formats created reach.

The result should be more than twelve posts. You should own a clearer library of founder beliefs, customer language, proof and reusable stories.

The secret is not the ghostwriter

I know a small number of highly trusted writers who can help leaders do this well. Their value is not secrecy. It is discretion, curiosity and the ability to hear the sentence underneath the first answer.

Whether you write alone or work with someone, the standard remains the same: useful truth, recognisable voice and evidence that helps the right customer make a better decision.
