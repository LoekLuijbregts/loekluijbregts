Stop Asking What to Post. Build a Story Inventory.

The content problem is rarely a lack of ideas. Valuable stories are being created every week, but nobody is collecting them while they are still specific and alive.

By Loek Luijbregts · March 13, 2026 · 8 minute read

“What should we post?” sounds like a creative question. In many businesses it is an evidence problem.

The team is trying to invent content on Thursday afternoon while valuable raw material was discarded all week: a customer objection, an unexpected result, a founder decision, a field photograph, a product detail or a sentence from a review.

A story inventory changes the job from invention to collection and selection.

The six shelves

1. Customer questions

Save the questions people ask before buying, during delivery and after receiving the result. A repeated question is proven demand for an explanation.

2. Customer language

Collect exact phrases from calls, reviews and emails. These reveal what customers notice and how they describe value when no marketer is helping them.

3. Proof

Results, rebookings, route clicks, leads, press coverage, introductions, time saved and before-and-after examples belong here. Add context so the number does not float without meaning.

4. Process

Document the choices and details that affect quality. Do not expose confidential methods. Show enough to help customers recognise the difference between careful work and a commodity.

5. Founder beliefs

Write down the industry assumptions you accept, reject or see differently. A belief becomes more valuable when attached to the experience that produced it.

6. Field notes

Save what you notice while visiting customers, testing products, travelling, speaking, creating or solving a real problem. Field notes prevent the brand from sounding like it lives inside a content workshop.

Minimum viable inventory

Create one shared document with six headings. Under every item, save the source, the date, the exact detail and why it might matter to a customer. A screenshot without context quickly becomes digital clutter.

Collect before you package

Do not force every observation into a finished post immediately. One customer sentence may later become:

  • a line on the homepage;
  • the opening of a LinkedIn post;
  • a sales objection in the FAQ;
  • a question for three more customers;
  • a proof point in a proposal;
  • or the premise of a longer article.

The inventory preserves optionality. Packaging comes after you understand what the material can prove.

A 30-day collection rhythm

Week 1: listen. Save ten customer questions and five exact phrases.

Week 2: inspect the work. Photograph five details and explain why each affects the result.

Week 3: gather proof. Add three outcomes, with source and commercial context.

Week 4: form a point of view. Write three beliefs supported by the material you collected.

At the end of the month, choose the stories that help ideal customers answer a real decision. Do not simply select the most dramatic.

What strong proof looks like

“9M impressions” is attention. “9M impressions and 270+ leads for €8,000+ bikes, delivered remotely with €0 ad spend” gives the audience a commercial result, customer context and delivery constraint.

Assign ownership close to the work

The marketing person cannot observe everything. Give customer-facing colleagues, founders and delivery teams a five-minute capture habit. The person nearest the evidence should save it. A writer or strategist can later help find the meaning.

This also makes founder-led writing easier. The founder is no longer expected to summon an original insight on demand. They can respond to a living archive of moments they actually experienced.

Measure whether the inventory improves the business

The first return is not reach. Look for better customer recognition: prospects repeating your language, sales calls starting further ahead, useful replies from ideal customers and teams reusing stories without asking the founder to explain them again.

Eventually, the inventory becomes strategic infrastructure. It informs positioning, proposition, media pitching, recruitment, partnerships and product decisions.

The best content calendar starts behind you

Look at the work already completed, the conversations already happening and the proof already earned. There is probably more valuable material there than in fifty generic prompts about what is trending.

Stop asking the internet what your business should say. Start noticing what your customers and work are already trying to tell you.
